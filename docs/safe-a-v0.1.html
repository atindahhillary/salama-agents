SAFE-A a field standard for secure AI agents in education and NGOs

Version 0.119 October 2026Licence: Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0)

SAFE-A sets out 20 controls that an organisation should have in place before an AI agent reads, drafts or acts on information about the people it serves. It is written for NGOs, schools, education programmes and the people who fund and build for them, in any country, and it is designed for real field conditions: patchy connectivity, shared phones, many languages and frequent staff turnover.

Principles

  • People first. The people an organisation serves, especially children, carry the risk of AI mistakes, so the controls protect them before they speed anyone up.
  • Least power. An agent gets the smallest set of data and tools its job needs, and anything that leaves the organisation passes a person.
  • Data, not orders. Content from outside the organisation can inform an agent but can never instruct it.
  • Prove it. Every control has evidence a manager, funder or auditor can check in an afternoon.
  • Built for the field. A control that only works with fast internet, new laptops and stable staff is not a control.

Scope

  • Any AI system an organisation uses that can read, draft, decide or act on information about the people it serves (participants, learners, patients, survivors, families) or on the organisation's own systems.
  • This includes agents that use tools, assistants built into everyday software, and general chat tools used for work.
  • It applies whoever built the system: the organisation itself, a vendor, a volunteer or a funder.

The 20 controls

Outside content informs an agent. It never gives orders.

FE-1Outside content is data, not instructions. Emails, messages, forms, documents and web pages can inform an agent but never instruct it. Prompts mark outside content as untrusted, in every language the agent reads.Prompt design notes and the latest injection test results.L1

EV · Evidence

Every action is logged, tested monthly, and covered by a plan for when things go wrong.

IDControlEvidenceLevel
EV-1Action log. Every agent action records when it happened, who or what triggered it, what it read (by reference), what it did and who approved it. Logs avoid raw personal data and are kept for an agreed period.A sample of the log.L1

EV-4Privacy risk check. Each agent that handles personal data has a privacy risk check (a data protection impact assessment where the law requires one) before it goes live and after any major change.The completed check.L2

It works offline, on the phones people have, in the languages they speak, and it survives staff turnover.

AD-1Degraded mode. Work continues when connectivity or the model service fails. The agent saves work locally, and the manual process stays documented.A test of the failure mode.L2

Levels

  • Level 1: Foundations. All 11 Level 1 controls are in place. An organisation at Level 1 may use AI for drafting on de-identified data, with a person reviewing every output.
  • Level 2: Pilot. All 20 controls are in place. An organisation at Level 2 may run a supervised pilot of one agent that handles personal data.
  • Level 3: Scale. All 20 controls have held through at least two monthly evaluations, and an independent reviewer has checked the evidence. An organisation at Level 3 may run several agents.

Evidence gates for child-facing use

No agent may talk directly with children, or show AI-generated content to a class, until every gate below is passed. Time alone proves nothing, so these are evidence gates.

  1. At least two school terms or six months of staff-facing use, with action logs and monthly evaluations, and no unresolved serious incident.
  2. A child-facing privacy risk check.
  3. Written sign-off from a named safeguarding lead.
  4. Opt-in consent from each child's parent or guardian, and the child's own assent, in a language they understand.
  5. Written notice to the relevant education or child protection authority.
  6. No AI-generated images of real learners, ever.

Definitions

Agent
An AI system that can use tools or take actions, such as sending a message, changing a record or calling another system.
Personal data
Any information about an identifiable person, including names, phone numbers, ID numbers, photos, locations and records.
Sensitive data
Children's data, and health, disability, case, survivor, financial and biometric information.
De-identified
Data with direct identifiers replaced by tokens or removed. It may still identify someone when combined with other facts, so it is handled with care.
Job card
A one-page description of an agent's purpose, data, permitted and forbidden actions, and owner.
Person in the loop
A named, trained person who approves an agent's action before it takes effect.
Residency record
A written record of where each tool processes and stores data, under which agreement.

How the readiness check scores

The Salama readiness check turns SAFE-A into a short self-assessment. Its scoring is published so anyone can check it.

  • Five pillar scores from 0 to 100, weighted Scope 20%, Anonymise 25%, Fence 20%, Evidence 20%, Adapt 15%.
  • A critical cap: shared logins, children's, health or case data in free AI tools, or learner records handled outside a school cap the overall score at 39 until fixed.
  • Four bands: Foundations first (0 to 39), Building (40 to 64), Pilot-ready (65 to 79), Scale-ready (80 and above).
  • "Not sure" answers are scored as gaps and listed as worth checking.
  • The score reflects self-reported answers. It is not an audit, a certification or a legal opinion.

Not law, not certification

SAFE-A is good practice, not law. It complements data protection, child protection and education laws but does not replace them, and meeting SAFE-A does not mean an organisation complies with any law. Where a law sets a stricter rule, the law wins. SAFE-A does not certify anyone, and no one may claim to be SAFE-A certified.

Case study

Designing a safe measurement spine: SAFE-A applied to SELution

Design-stage review. No learner data was used, and no outcomes are claimed.

SELution is a play-based programme for low-resource classrooms that delivers four connected strands, social and emotional learning, foundational literacy and numeracy, unplugged coding and robotics, through one teacher-training pathway and one measurement spine. It is at the design and partnership stage, with no funded pilot yet.

What SELution plans

  • Every session follows one cycle: goal, game, roles, reflect, record.
  • The teacher completes a record for each child in about ten seconds. These per-child records roll up to a cluster dashboard.
  • A pilot covers one district or county cluster of 8 to 15 schools for one to two terms, with a baseline and an endline assessment.
  • Materials are deliberately cheap, so most spend goes to teacher coaching.

Why it matters

That design makes SELution a child-data system from the first session. Per-child records, parents' phone numbers, cluster dashboards and WhatsApp coordination are exactly where children's data tends to leak, and they are also where AI is most tempting: summarising progress, drafting session plans and writing to parents.

What SAFE-A recommends

Scope

Three job cards instead of one general assistant: a Teacher Planning Assistant that drafts session plans and never sees learner data (Tier 1); a Cluster Summary Agent that reads aggregate counts only (Tier 0); and a Parent Message Assistant that drafts messages a teacher checks and sends (Tier 2).

Anonymise

Give each child a pseudonymous code at baseline and keep names on the school's own register. The dashboard works at group level, figures for groups smaller than five are hidden, and AI sees counts, never rows. Decide the identifier scheme before the baseline, because it is hard to change afterwards.

Fence

Parents' replies on WhatsApp never reach an agent that can act. Messages are drafted for a teacher and never sent automatically.

Evidence

A coach reviews a sample of AI-drafted session plans against the curriculum every month. Every draft that is accepted, edited or rejected is logged, and a breach plan with named roles is signed before the baseline.

Adapt

Records are captured on paper or a shared tablet and synced at cluster level, so a lost connection never loses a session. Materials are tested with teachers in both English and Kiswahili, and the dashboard and documentation are handed over to the implementing partner so the system survives staff and funder changes.

What a pilot would measure

  • Teacher minutes saved per week, logged by the teachers themselves.
  • Session-plan quality, scored blind by a coach against a curriculum checklist.
  • The share of AI drafts rejected or heavily edited.
  • Data incidents, with a target of zero.
  • Parent response to drafted messages.

Lessons from the design stage

  1. Child data starts on day one. A ten-second record per child creates thousands of records in a term, so the controls have to come before the first session, not after the dashboard is built.
  2. Aggregates first. Most of the AI value in this design comes from summaries that never need a name.
  3. The cheapest control is not collecting. Every field on the per-child record should earn its place against the evidence threshold the pilot has to meet.
